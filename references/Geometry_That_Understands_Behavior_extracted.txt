Executive abstract
Modern representation learning often places complex systems into low-dimensional vector spaces and compares
the resulting vectors by Euclidean or cosine distance. Those distances are convenient, but generally do not encode
physical energy, response sensitivity, dynamics, or intervention effects. Structural mechanics offers a constructive
alternative: a symmetric positive-definite stiffness matrix defines a quadratic energy, and therefore a physically
interpretable norm on displacement differences. Graph signal processing, system identification, optimal control,
information geometry, and Riemannian representation learning provide related constructions.
This monograph develops the connection carefully. For a spring network, the assembled stiffness matrix is
algebraically the weighted graph Laplacian. For a linear dynamical system, the finite-horizon observability Gramian
weights initial-state differences by the resulting sensor-output differences. For a learned response model, a
Jacobian pullback gives a local positive-semidefinite metric that quantifies which embedding directions most
strongly change predicted behavior. Fractional powers of graph operators allow continuous reweighting of spectral
scales. Structural and behavioral metrics can be combined—subject to compatibility, scaling, positivity, experimental
identification, and rigorous validation—to create domain-specific embedding geometries.


The document distinguishes mathematical identities, engineering analogies, established research approaches, and
new testable proposals. It provides exact calculations, a proposed architecture, an implementation sketch, a
structural-engineering benchmark, a possible application to single-cell perturbation prediction, statistical validation
rules, ablation studies, limitations, and a reproducible Python demonstration.


Five essential conclusions
  1.    A distance is meaningful only relative to what must be preserved. Different scientific questions require
        different geometries.
  2.    For a fixed linear elastic structure, the stiffness-energy norm is exact; for nonlinear, stochastic, or learned
        systems, analogous operators must be derived and validated.
  3.    The output sensitivity matrix J ⊤ W J is a principled local geometric construction, but it is usually
        semidefinite, not automatically a globally valid distance.
  4.    Fractional powers Lα are a legitimate multiscale spectral family. They are not, by themselves, proof that a
        dataset has fractal structure.
  5.    The credible contribution is not inventing quadratic metrics. It is demonstrating that a carefully defined
        behavior-aware geometry improves out-of-sample scientific tasks beyond simpler representations, without
        data leakage or circular training targets.

This makes a hidden assumption: one unit of movement in every embedding direction has the same meaning, after
taking account of scale. That assumption is often unsupported. One coordinate can encode a nearly irrelevant
nuisance variable; another can strongly affect failure probability, cell response, vibration amplitude, or operational
decisions. A learned space can also be rotated, stretched, or reparameterized while preserving predictions, so raw
coordinate distance is generally not intrinsic.
Research question. Can we construct or learn embedding spaces such that proximity corresponds to a domain-
relevant notion of physical or functional equivalence?


Main hypothesis H1. A metric induced by physically meaningful operators or experimentally measured responses
predicts unseen behavior more faithfully than Euclidean distance between otherwise comparable embeddings.


Secondary hypothesis H2. Multiscale spectral weighting may recover useful differences that a single-scale energy
norm overlooks, but only when the underlying graph genuinely captures relevant organization.


Null hypothesis. Once representation dimension, supervision, model capacity, normalization, and computational
budget are controlled, metric-aware representations do not provide a reproducible improvement over simple
baselines.


These hypotheses are falsifiable. Failure of H1 or H2 is informative and should not be reinterpreted as a successful
demonstration of the proposal.

                                                                                                       2
A nonlinear structure has potential energy Π ( u ) and local tangent stiffness K t ( u ) =∇ Π ( u ) where differentiable.
A positive-definite K t defines a local quadratic metric in a stable region, but not necessarily one global metric.

Geodesic length in a positive local metric should also not be confused with the actual mechanical work along a
nonlinear loading path.

The scalar spring-network stiffness is precisely this operator when edge weights represent spring stiffness. In graph

6.1 Important mathematical qualifications
   •     The combinatorial Laplacian of any graph has at least one zero eigenvalue. A connected graph has the
         constant vector in its nullspace; a graph with c connected components has at least c independent null
         vectors.
   •     Therefore   √ Δ x ⊤ L Δ x is normally a pseudometric on raw node signals. It becomes a metric on the
         quotient space after declaring constant shifts on each connected component equivalent, or on an
         appropriately constrained subspace.
   •     Adding ε I produces a positive-definite metric but changes the scientific meaning: even constant shifts
         become costly. This should be declared, not hidden.
   •     A degree-normalized Laplacian and a combinatorial Laplacian give different quadratic energies. Their
         choice should be an experimental factor.
   •     Directed, signed, inhibitory, or nonconservative interactions require additional modeling. Simply
         substituting a nonsymmetric adjacency matrix can destroy the properties used above.

6.2 Graph smoothness is not automatically system behavior
Low graph energy means neighboring nodes have similar values. It does not automatically imply biological
regulatory similarity, mechanical stability, or causal equivalence. A biological coexpression edge reflects a statistical

8. A global Mahalanobis metric versus a physical metric
A general global quadratic distance is

The meaning of the distance depends on five explicit choices:

   2.    Observable: which outputs are scientifically or operationally important?

   5.    Uncertainty: how much measurement noise and model error should count?

12.2 The two main failure modes
First, a model can be wrong. If g ϕ predicts an incorrect response, then its Jacobian defines a confidently wrong
geometry. The metric is only as trustworthy as the response model and its training evidence. Validation against
genuinely unseen responses is mandatory.
Second, a Jacobian sees local first-order behavior. In regions of saturation, discontinuity, bifurcation, or large state
changes, the local quadratic approximation can be inadequate. A path-integrated geometry or direct finite-
response comparison may be more appropriate.

If M B depends on z , distance is not computed reliably by using only the matrix at the starting point. A

An important existing body of work studies these geometries for learned generative models [R5, R6]. This proposal
belongs in that intellectual tradition, rather than claiming that Jacobian-based latent metrics are new.

15.1 Multiscale does not mean fractal
A structure can exhibit behavior at several scales without having a measurable fractal dimension. A claim of
fractality requires an operational definition, a defensible scaling range, uncertainty estimates, and comparison with
nonfractal alternatives. For a finite graph, apparent power laws can result from finite-size effects, degree
heterogeneity, thresholding, or preprocessing.
We therefore propose two separate research tracks:


   1.    Spectral-metric track: Does adjusting spectral weighting improve the prediction task?
   2.    Scale-invariance track: Is there statistically credible evidence for stable scaling laws over enough
         independent scales to justify a fractal interpretation?
The first can succeed while the second fails. That would still be scientifically valuable.


16. The general principle: define meaning before learning coordinates
We can now express the guiding method:
Step 1 — Specify the behavioral equivalence relation. Two states are equivalent for the application when their
responses agree within a meaningful tolerance under the approved conditions.


Step 2 — Choose structural and response operators. These may be an assembled stiffness matrix, a verified graph
Laplacian, an observability Gramian, a response function, or a probability model.


Step 3 — Pull the chosen geometry into a representation space. Use analytical reduction, Jacobian pullback, or
direct pairwise response distances.


Step 4 — Test whether the metric preserves or predicts unseen outcomes. A plausible-looking embedding
visualization is insufficient.


Step 5 — Interpret the scale and null directions. What counts as indistinguishable? Which perturbations produce
large changes? What is intentionally ignored?


In short: rather than declaring that two objects are similar because their vectors are close, construct a space in
which they are close because an explicit scientific relationship is preserved.

19. Causality: an aspiration that needs stronger evidence
A behavior-aware metric is not automatically a causal metric. Predicting outcomes conditional on s is
observational if the data do not identify the effect of intervening on s .

A causal interpretation requires an explicit estimand and assumptions, such as the interventional response
E [ Y ∣ do ( S=s ) , X =x ), and suitable experimental assignment or a credible identification strategy. Mechanistic
simulators can supply interventions by construction, subject to simulator validity. Biological perturbation
experiments may directly manipulate a target, but still require controls for off-target effects, selection, batch, dose,
and measurement noise.


We therefore distinguish:


   •     Response-aware: similar under observed or predicted input–output behavior.
   •     Intervention-aware: similar under experimentally manipulated conditions or identified causal effects.
   •     Causal-structure-aware: sensitive to the mechanisms generating those effects; an even stronger claim
         requiring additional evidence.
The first may be demonstrated with observational predictive data; the others require more disciplined evidence.


20. Algorithms, numerical complexity, and implementation choices
20.1 Small problems: exact matrices
For small matrices, use symmetric eigendecomposition, verify eigenvalues, compute Lα , and evaluate quadratic
distances. Dense eigendecomposition is roughly cubic in node count and should not be used indiscriminately on
large graphs.

For fixed global metrics, use Cholesky or spectral factorization M =R ⊤ R and compute Euclidean distances
between transformed vectors. Ensure all data types and numerical tolerances are explicit.


20.2 Large graphs: avoid materializing dense fractional operators
A sparse graph Laplacian can become dense after a fractional matrix power. For tens of thousands of nodes, an
exact n × n dense representation is generally unattractive in memory and compute.

signal being sought. Approximation error must be quantified on representative vectors.

Part V — Experiments that could establish or refute value
21. Experiment A: finite-element structures with known governing equations
Objective. Establish that the metric matches known mechanical-response relationships and adds value beyond a
naive representation—not merely that a stiffness norm produces familiar results.

21.1 Build the dataset
Start with small two-dimensional trusses or shear-frame models whose stiffness matrices can be assembled exactly.

   •       Record topology, node positions, cross-sections, elastic moduli, supports, and admissible loading points.

21.4 The split must prevent near-duplicate leakage
For a generalization test, separate structure families or topology templates between training, development, and
final test, or explicitly test both within-topology and out-of-topology conditions. Merely generating multiple load

distance oracle. Declare whether the test targets interpolation within an observed load family or extrapolation to a
new family.


21.5 Primary and secondary outcomes
Preselect one primary outcome, such as Spearman correlation between model distance and independently
computed oracle response-profile distance over test pairs drawn from held-out structures. Pair dependence must
be accounted for in inference: pairs sharing a structure are not independent.

Secondary outcomes may include top- k neighbor retrieval precision, prediction of responses for unseen load cases,
ranking of structures by reliability-related outcomes, runtime, sensitivity to measurement noise, and robustness
across topology families.


A meaningful result must show task relevance. A distance that correlates with simulator response but does not
improve any downstream decision may still be mathematically interesting, but its engineering value is narrower
than claimed.

In particular: do not incorporate final-test labels into the graph, the target-distance oracle, pair selection, spectral
scale sweep, early stopping, or metric selection. Preserve the accepted submission and its artifacts untouched.
Experimental branches should record input hashes, code revision, preprocessing rules, metric definitions, seeds,
and final decisions.


24. Statistical validity, negative controls, and falsification
A credible result requires more than an improved mean number from one experiment.

24.1 Units of replication
In structural experiments, the independent unit may be a structure family or independently generated design, not
every load case or every pair sharing the same design. In cell perturbation studies, many cells can belong to the
same perturbation group; treating each cell as an independent replication of the perturbation effect causes
pseudoreplication.

25. Research risks and non-obvious failure modes
25.1 Units and interpretation
A metric can inadvertently combine millimeters of deflection, frequency in hertz, and stresses in megapascals.
Without physical normalization or an explicitly justified utility function, the largest numerical scale dominates. The
same problem appears with gene counts, probabilities, and effect sizes. Document transformations, weights, and
units at every stage.

25.2 Nullspaces and identifiability
A graph Laplacian ignores connected-component constants; some sensors ignore entire mechanical modes; a
response model may ignore latent directions. Zero distance does not always mean equal physical states. The
equivalence class must match intended use. Blind ε I regularization can conceal nonidentifiability rather than solve
it scientifically.

25.3 Uncertainty in operator construction
A mechanical stiffness matrix may be based on uncertain material properties or boundary conditions. A graph
inferred from control data may change substantially with preprocessing, sampling density, and batch effects.
Propagate or at least stress-test those uncertainties rather than treating the operator as ground truth.

25.4 Metric collapse and learned shortcuts
A trainable encoder can collapse meaningful variation or exploit confounding features. A response predictor can
learn shortcuts whose Jacobian is numerically smooth but scientifically incorrect. Test on interventions, unseen
environments, and controlled changes of nuisance variables where possible.

25.5 Out-of-distribution extrapolation
Jacobian sensitivity describes local movement around observed states. Far outside the training manifold, local
metric tensors can be unreliable and geodesic paths may travel through unrealistic regions of latent space. A
geodesic optimization should enforce or penalize low-support regions if such paths are considered physically
meaningful.

25.6 Computational cost and approximate spectra
Fractional graph operators may be dense. Jacobian metrics may scale poorly with response dimension. Geodesic
path optimization is nonconvex and can be sensitive to initialization. If compute overwhelms the benefit, the
research should favor a simpler fixed metric or response-distance model.

25.7 Incorrect claims of novelty or causality
Matrix-weighted norms, graph Dirichlet energies, Gramian-based system comparison, spectral fractional powers,
metric learning, and pullback Riemannian geometry are established. A new contribution must be specified narrowly
—for example, a particular composite objective, an identified equivalence relation, a provable property under
assumptions, or a robust benchmark gain. A response-trained representation is not automatically causal.

27. What would count as a scientific contribution?
A paper or research artifact would need a narrower, defensible claim than “embeddings can use stiffness-like
matrices,” because that mathematical pattern is known.
Promising contribution types include:


C1. Theory. Prove an equivalence or approximation bound between a constrained physical-response distance and a
learned low-dimensional metric for a defined model class.


C2. Method. Construct a scalable combined graph-response metric with mathematically valid positivity and a
principled treatment of nullspaces, normalization, and uncertainty.


C3. Empirical science. Show that a fixed fractional-spectral feature group captures held-out intervention behavior
not explained by conventional spectral embeddings, graph neural networks, or a strong predictive baseline.


C4. Interpretability. Show that dominant eigenvectors of the learned metric correspond to independently verified
mechanical or biological response mechanisms rather than merely visually plausible axes.


C5. Robustness. Demonstrate that the metric tracks relevant behavior under changing load distributions, sensor
availability, graph uncertainty, and dataset shifts more reliably than simpler distances.


The first research report should state which one of these it is targeting, what existing literature is closest, and
which failure result would refute the targeted claim.

29. Interpretation of results: three possible outcomes
Outcome A: structural distances work, but learned metrics add little. That would validate the physical intuition
while showing that additional neural complexity is unnecessary for this problem class.
Outcome B: learned response metrics improve held-out behavior prediction. That would support the idea that
scientifically chosen response geometry contributes beyond coordinate compression and global reweighting,
especially if the effect survives negative controls and new load families.


Outcome C: fractional features help only on selected contexts. This would suggest conditional multiscale
structure. The appropriate follow-up would be to identify the context differences, repeat the result independently,
and assess whether scale choices can be predicted from training-only information.


Each outcome advances knowledge. A research design that can only declare success is not a credible test.

30. Final synthesis
The most useful insight is not the observation that mechanics and AI both use matrices. It is a change in the
definition of similarity.
An ordinary embedding answers: “Which objects have nearby coordinates in the learned representation?”


A physics-aware embedding asks: “Which differences are energetically important under the governing model?”


A behavior-aware embedding asks: “Which differences cause measurably different outputs under the conditions
that matter?”


An intervention-aware representation asks: “Which differences persist when the system is actively changed?”


A transition-cost representation asks: “How hard is it to move from one state to another?”


These are not interchangeable questions. Their mathematically appropriate operators include stiffness matrices,
graph Laplacians, observability Gramians, Jacobian pullback tensors, information metrics, and control costs.


The unifying proposal is to choose a scientifically meaningful response space first, derive or estimate its geometry
second, and only then learn latent coordinates that respect it. The mathematical construction is credible; its
practical benefit must be established one application and one clean experiment at a time.

B.1 Small-matrix stiffness distance
  import numpy as np

  K = np.array([[2.0, -1.0], [-1.0, 1.0]])
  a = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
  b = np.array([1.0, -1.0])

  def energy_distance(x, y, K):
      delta = x - y
      return np.sqrt(delta @ K @ delta)

  assert np.isclose(energy_distance(a, np.zeros(2), K), 1.0)
  assert np.isclose(energy_distance(b, np.zeros(2), K), np.sqrt(5))
  assert np.isclose(energy_distance(a, b, K), 2.0)


B.2 Small-matrix fractional Laplacian
  import numpy as np

  def fractional_laplacian(L, alpha):
      eigvals, Q = np.linalg.eigh(L)
      if np.min(eigvals) < -1e-10:
          raise ValueError("Not positive semidefinite")
      eigvals = np.clip(eigvals, 0.0, None)
      return (Q * eigvals**alpha) @ Q.T

  B = np.array([[1., -1., 0.], [0., 1., -1.]])
  L = B.T @ B
  for alpha in (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0):
      La = fractional_laplacian(L, alpha)
      assert np.linalg.eigvalsh(La).min() > -1e-9
      assert np.allclose(La @ np.ones(3), np.zeros(3), atol=1e-9)
These exact routines are appropriate for tiny demonstration matrices only. The main text explains why sparse
matrix-function approximations are needed at scale.


B.3 Verify a Jacobian pullback without training a neural network
  import numpy as np

  J = np.array([[1.0, 0.2], [0.0, 3.0]])
  W = np.diag([1.0, 0.25])
  M = J.T @ W @ J

  delta = np.array([0.0, 0.5])
  latent_quadratic = delta @ M @ delta
  output_quadratic = (J @ delta) @ W @ (J @ delta)
  assert np.isclose(latent_quadratic, output_quadratic)
  assert np.isclose(latent_quadratic, 0.5725)
